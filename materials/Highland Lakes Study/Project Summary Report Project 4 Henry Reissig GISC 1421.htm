Executive Summary

With extensive growth in the central Texas region, the Highland Lakes serve as a vital source of water for much of the region. The Highland Lakes are a chain of dammed water reservoirs along the Colorado River. The Lower Colorado River Authority (LCRA) manages the dams and the hydropower off them. The LCRA officials want a change detection analysis done for the Highland Lakes throughout the years. For this study, the two years picked for the before and after are 1987 and 2011.

To document this change, the land cover needs to be first classified. Five classifications with the designated codes are used in this study are: (0) Water, (1) Woodland, (2) Grassland, (3) Crops, (4) Developed, and (5) Barren. Training samples were then created. For this study, 30 training samples were created for all five classifications.

Supervised classification was then performed where the user groups pixels with similar spectral signatures. The software then performed supervised classification based on the training samples on the Landsat images for both 1987 and 2011. The overall accuracy of the supervised classification for 1987 is 33.3% and for 2011, it’s 43.3%.

Unsupervised classification was then performed where the software groups pixels with similar spectral signatures into classes defined by the user. A land cover classification is then assigned after analyzing each classification group for both 1987 and 2011. The overall accuracy of the supervised classification for 1987 is 53.3% and for 2011, it’s 40.0%.

Visually, a vast difference can be seen between 1987 and 2011. The developed classification is much more prevalent in both the supervised and unsupervised model in 2011 as compared to 1987.

This visual change was then analyzed for land cover, water and vegetation. As shown in figure. For the change in land cover, the total area of water dropped by 32%, woodlands dropped by 2%, grassland increased by 121.6%, crops decreased by 64%, developed increased by 27% and barren land increased by 14%. This is shown in Figures 7 and 8. The drought of 2011 was quite evident in the water analysis. For the change in water depth, what is considered deep water down from 89% to 61%, shallow water went up from 6% to 10% and dry went from 5% to 29%. This is shown in Figures 9, 10 and 11. The change in vegetation showed a loss with an average of -5%. The map showing this (Figure 13) and the distribution of values shows how even the pluses and minuses are (Figure 14).

 

Summary of Approach

Change detection analysis was done with the following tasks: collect and assimilate project data, analyze spectral signatures, classify land cover, and perform accuracy assessment. Data was gathered from the USGS’s Earth Explorer. Landsat images for both 1987 and 2011 were extracted from this website after selecting the study area around Highland Lakes and project to NAD 1983 UTM Zone 14N to the project geodatabase. Five classifications with the designated codes are used in this study are: (0) Water, (1) Woodland, (2) Grassland, (3) Crops, (4) Developed, and (5) Barren. For this study, 30 random sample points were created. Spectral signatures were produced from these samples as shown in Figure 1.

Created a new schema based on the National Land Cover Database (2011) (NLCD2011) classification schema. The new schema was based on the five classification codes stated above. This classification schema was saved and used for the rest of the project. Training samples were then created for all five of the land cover classes for this project.

Supervised classification was then performed where the user groups pixels with similar spectral signatures. The software then performed supervised classification based on the training samples on the Landsat images for both 1987 and 2011. Figure 2 and Figure 3 show the years 1987 and 2011 respectively.

Unsupervised classification was then performed where the software groups pixels with similar spectral signatures into classes defined by the user. A land cover classification is then assigned after analyzing each classification group for both 1987 and 2011. Figure 4 and Figure 5 show the years 1987 and 2011 respectively.  The accuracy assessment is covered in the Executive Summary and Accuracy Assessment section of this document.

To analyze the change between 1987 and 2011, the supervised classification model will be used. The Majority Filter and Boundary Clean geoprocessing tools were used to generalize and smooth the land cover surface. To analyze the change in land cover, the change was initially analyzed using the swipe tool to see the changes by going back and forth while zooming in various areas. A data table was then created from the attribute table created after the generalization and smoothing process. This attribute table has the number of pixels for each of the land cover classes. Knowing that each pixel represents a 30 meter by 30-meter square the total pixels can then be converted to square miles leading to Figures 7 and Figure 8.

To analyze the change in water, the Near Infrared (NIR) for both 1987 and 2011 images are used. Extract the Highland Lake study area using the Highland Lakes layers. Then use the slice tool to classify the water by depth by natural breaks for both the 1987 (Figure 9) and 2011 (Figure 10) layers. Using the attribute table for this step, and knowing that each pixel is 30 meters, the area in square miles for the three classes of depth are calculated and shown in Figure 11 and Figure 12.

To analyze the change in vegetation, the TM multispectral images are used for 1987 and 2011 that were used earlier in the project. In the band combination tool, color infrared is chosen to create red, green, blue band combination. From there, the NDVI (Normal Difference Vegetation Index) was calculated using the NDVI Colorized function for both 1987 and 2011. The minus function was then used between 1987 and 2011 to generate the map on Figure 13.

Conclusion

Based on the accuracy assessment, the overall accuracy was very similar between supervised and unsupervised classification with a slight advantage going towards unsupervised classification. The process of classifying all 30 training samples was challenging by going back and forth between the Landsat imagery. With a resolution of 30 meters, this is very difficult to do, so the ArcGIS World Imagery was used with it’s one meter resolution. The problem is that this is current and what we are analyzing is either 1987 or 2011. Later, it is shown through error matrix that this will affect the producer’s and user’s accuracy since these sample data points will be used as the training or reference data. A couple of ways to address this would be to follow the Statement of Work (SOW)’s suggestion of using Landsat 5 images and experimenting with a variety of band combinations. Also, the SOW suggested using USGS NLCD and TPWD Ecological Mapping Systems land cover datasets to help as well.  In all, despite the issues going through the process, the main point of the study to see the change between 1987 and 2011 was clear. More iterations will improve the process with time.

The general takeaway from the change analysis is that the land cover change points towards a great loss in crop lands and a marked increase in development during that time. Surprisingly, woodlands did not change significantly during that time. More analysis needed to be performed on exactly what land cover types changed, but one could make a case that much of the croplands changed to developed, barren or grasslands during that time.
